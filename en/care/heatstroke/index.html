Safety and emergencies

Heatstroke in dogs and cats during Egypt's summer: signs, first aid and prevention

Updated: 30 October 2026 · 4 min read

General information only. It does not replace an examination by a vet who knows your pet. In an emergency, go to the nearest clinic straight away.

Dogs and cats don't sweat like people do. A dog cools down mainly by panting, and loses a little heat through its paw pads. When heat or humidity is high — as it often is in an Egyptian summer — this may not be enough, and body temperature rises quickly. Heatstroke is an emergency that can damage the brain, kidneys, gut and blood clotting, and can kill within a short time.

Who is most at risk?

  • Flat-faced breeds such as Pugs, Bulldogs and Shih Tzus, and Persian cats, because their airways are narrow.
  • Overweight and older pets, and very young puppies.
  • Thick-coated pets and breeds from cold climates.
  • Pets with heart or breathing problems.
  • Active dogs after running or rough play in the heat.

Common summer mistakes

  • Leaving a pet in the car, even for minutes with the windows slightly open. The temperature in a closed car rises very fast, even in the shade.
  • Leaving a dog on the roof or balcony in the sun all day without shade and water.
  • Tying a dog up in the open where it can't move into shade.
  • Walking at midday on hot asphalt, exposing the dog to heat and burnt paw pads.

The asphalt test: put the back of your hand on the ground. If you can't hold it there for 7 seconds, it is too hot for your pet's paws.

Signs of heatstroke

  • heavy, constant panting that doesn't settle with rest;
  • thick, sticky drool;
  • dark red gums and tongue — or pale or bluish ones in later stages;
  • a fast heartbeat;
  • vomiting or diarrhoea, sometimes with blood;
  • staggering, confusion, weakness and not responding when called;
  • collapse, seizures or loss of consciousness.

In cats, open-mouth panting is abnormal in itself and needs immediate action in hot weather.

First aid on the way to the vet

  1. Move your pet straight away to a shaded or air-conditioned place.
  2. Pour cool — not ice-cold — water over its body, especially the neck, belly, armpits, groin and paws.
  3. Use a fan or the air conditioning along with the water; evaporation helps cooling.
  4. Don't leave a wet towel lying on your pet, as it can trap heat. You can wipe a wet towel over the body and keep re-wetting it.
  5. Offer small sips of water if your pet is alert and able to swallow; don't force it.
  6. Don't use ice or iced water: it can make blood vessels in the skin narrow and slow down cooling.
  7. Go to the vet straight away, continuing to cool your pet in the air-conditioned car on the way.

Even if your pet seems better after cooling, a vet check is essential, because organ damage and clotting problems can appear hours later.

Prevention in summer

  • Walk early in the morning or after sunset.
  • Provide cool, clean water, shade and ventilation at all times, with more than one bowl.
  • Cut down rough play on hot days.
  • A cooling mat or cool tiled floor gives your pet somewhere to lie.
  • Keep your pet at a healthy weight; obesity raises the risk of heatstroke.
  • Brush regularly to remove dead hair. Shaving double-coated breeds down to the skin is usually not recommended.
  • Take extra care in humid coastal cities, where high humidity makes panting less effective.

Frequently asked questions

Should I shave my dog to help it cope with the heat?

Not always. A double coat protects from the sun and insulates against heat, and shaving to the skin can expose it to sunburn. Ask your vet or groomer about your dog's breed.

How long can I leave my dog in the car with the air conditioning on?

It's best never to leave it alone in the car in summer at all — the engine or air conditioning can stop without you noticing.

Does ice help cool a pet down?

It isn't recommended. Ordinary cool water with moving air is the best and safest option until you reach the vet.
